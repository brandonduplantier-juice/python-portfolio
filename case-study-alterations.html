Case Study - Client Analytics

Eight Years of Traffic, and the One Signal the Campaigns Left Behind

Alterations by Josetta, 2018 to 2025. Duplantier Advertising.

The engagement

Alterations by Josetta is a single-location alterations and custom tailoring business. I have run the analytics side since 2018: I built and maintain the website, configured the tracking, and produce the reporting the owner uses to decide when and where to spend.

This is not a dataset exercise. Every number here came from a business that had to keep the lights on, and the decisions it drove cost real money.

The question

An alterations business is seasonal in a way the owner feels but cannot quantify. Prom, weddings, graduations, and the fall return to work all pull demand. The dead months in between are dead every year.

The owner's question was never "how much traffic do we get." It was when should I spend, and on what. That is a timing question before it is a media buying question, and it is answerable from the traffic record.

Three phases. Growth through 2019. Then 2020, which the annual total understates: the monthly record shows February running near 180 visits, March collapsing to roughly 12, and April and May sitting at zero. Not low. Zero. An in-person alterations business stopped existing for two months, the year closed 27 percent down, and 2021 nearly doubled off that floor. Then a plateau: 2022, 2023, and 2024 average 2,510 visits and go nowhere for three years.

2025 broke the plateau by 20 percent above that three-year average. That is the year worth explaining.

The intervention

The site's contact form had been quietly accumulating a list. It now stands at 399 contacts, every one of them someone who found the business and raised their hand.

The traffic record shows the same shape every year: a February floor, an April peak roughly two and a half times higher, and a second lift around late summer. Those peaks are not mysteries. They are prom, wedding season, graduations, and the return to school and work.

So the plan was straightforward and it used an asset the business already had: email the existing list against the calendar rather than against the budget. Campaigns went out ahead of prom, ahead of wedding season, ahead of sports and school events, and deliberately into the slow months where the traffic floor was lowest and the business had capacity sitting idle.

The signal it left behind

Email campaigns are hard to see in web analytics. A recipient who already knows the business does not arrive via a search result. They type the name, or click a link that carries no referrer. Both land in Direct.

In 2024, Google led at 1,141 visits with Direct just behind at 1,135. In 2025 that inverted. Direct rose 22 percent to 1,388 and became the largest source, while Google moved only 5 percent. The growth did not come from search. It came from people who already knew the business.

That is the shape an email program leaves in a traffic report when nothing else changed. It is consistent with the intervention, and it is not proof of it.

What this analysis cannot tell you

The conversion happened on the phone. The owner books alterations by call and walk-in, so there is no checkout, no form-to-sale link, and no revenue in the analytics platform. Squarespace reports zero dollars and zero checkouts for every year here, and that is correct, because the money never touched the website.

The email sends themselves were built outside the platform and those records are no longer accessible, so open rates, click rates, and send dates are gone.

Which means the Direct increase is an association. It is consistent with the campaign timing, it appeared in the year the campaigns ran hardest, and no competing explanation is visible in the data. It is not a causal finding and I will not present it as one.

Two things I would instrument differently

  • Close the phone gap first. A tracked call number on the site, swapped per campaign, would have turned the entire outcome from inferred to measured. It is cheap, it is available on every major platform, and its absence is the single biggest hole in eight years of otherwise clean reporting.
  • Tag the campaign links. UTM parameters on every email link would have split campaign traffic out of Direct entirely, and the argument above would have been a number instead of an inference.

Both are things I know now that I did not build in 2018. The honest version of this case study is that the strategy worked and the measurement did not keep up with it.

Things the data surfaced along the way

  • Bing went from 26 visits to 309 in a single year, a twelvefold jump against a flat Google. That is too large and too isolated to be organic growth in Bing's market share, and it is worth investigating before anyone builds a plan on it.
  • Bounce rate has climbed every year since 2019, from 35.4 percent to 51.4 percent, while traffic grew. More visitors, less engagement per visitor. Growth is masking a content problem.
  • The Wardrobe Seamstress page bounces at 91.9 percent on 563 views, the worst page on the site by a wide margin.
  • The 2022 figures disagree with each other. The summary reports 2,587 visits and the device breakdown reports 2,597 for the identical period. Ten apart, in the platform's own numbers. Small, but reconciliation is how you find out whether a source can be trusted, and this one cannot be fully.

What I take from it

The analysis that mattered here was not complicated. It was noticing that the business owned an asset it was not using, that the calendar was more predictable than the budget, and that the slow months were the ones worth spending into rather than the ones worth retreating from.

The part I would do differently is not the analysis. It is that I did not build the measurement to prove it.
