Client Work - Google Analytics - Case Study

Eight Years of Client Analytics: Alterations by Josetta

The only paying client on this page, and the one I have worked with longest. Since 2018 I have handled the analytics for a local alterations and tailoring shop: I built the website, set up the tracking, and produce the reports the owner uses to decide where her money goes. Visitors grew from 927 a year in 2018 to 3,020 in 2025, more than three times as many, and that includes 2020, when the shop went to zero website visitors for two straight months during the shutdown. The site had quietly collected a list of 399 people who reached out. We started emailing that list around the calendar instead of around the budget, going out ahead of prom and wedding season and deliberately during the slow months. After that, more visitors were arriving by typing the shop's name directly than by finding it on Google, for the first time ever. The full write-up explains what that does and does not prove, since the actual bookings happened over the phone where nothing was tracked.

What the Readmissions Program Cannot See

Medicare fines hospitals that send too many patients home only to have them come back within a month. This looks at the government's own results file for 3,055 hospitals and asks whether the scoring actually works. Three things stood out. More than a third of the records have no score at all, because a hospital needs at least 25 patients with a condition before Medicare will grade it, and that rule quietly removes most small and rural hospitals from the program's view entirely. About half of all graded hospitals are always going to land on the wrong side of the line, because each one is scored against the others rather than against a fixed target, so the fines never run out no matter how much care improves nationally. And a hospital that scores badly on one condition is barely more likely to score badly on another, which means the program treats six loosely related things as if they were one measure of hospital quality. Built with SQL and Python, with the analysis code published alongside it.

Hospital Readmission Analysis: Six Implementations

When a patient is sent home from the hospital and comes back within 30 days, that is a readmission, and hospitals are penalized for having too many. This project asks which patients are most at risk, and answers it six separate times in six different tools, to show the answer does not change depending on which software you use. The data is a 300-patient practice set built to mimic the real Medicare program. The finding: heart failure patients came back about 20 times more often than hip and knee replacement patients, the lowest-risk group. Scheduling a follow-up appointment looked like it helped, but not by enough to be sure, so it is reported that way instead of claimed as a result.

Aging Clock: Interactive Biological-Age Dashboard

Your body has an age that can differ from the number on your birthday. This dashboard estimates it from a blood sample and lets you see what drives the estimate: move a slider and watch the predicted age change in real time. On people the model had never seen, it lands within about 5.7 years of true age, and on a completely separate group of 689 people it holds at 6.4 years. That second number is the honest one, and it is the one I lead with.

NHANES Biological Age & Mortality Dashboard

Does a body that looks older than its years actually mean a shorter life? I tested that on 28,510 US adults tracked by the government for up to 19 years, using nine routine blood tests that any doctor's office runs. The answer is yes, and it is measurable: each extra year of biological age carried about 4.5 percent higher risk of dying, and it predicted survival better than a person's actual age did. Filter by age and sex and explore the survival curves yourself.

Longevity Intervention Evidence Explorer

Everyone has heard that some supplement or fasting protocol will help you live longer. This tool grades those claims on how strong the evidence actually is, using the same six criteria for every one of them, and shows the gap between how much attention something gets and how much proof stands behind it. Nothing earns the top grade, because the top grade requires a study that followed humans for a lifetime, and no such study exists yet. Every source links back to the published research.

Epigenetic Aging Clock: DNA Methylation Predictor

The code behind the aging clock dashboard. It reads roughly 450,000 chemical markers along the DNA of 656 people, then narrows that down on its own to the 176 that matter most for predicting age, and gets within about 5.7 years. As a check, the markers it picked overlap heavily with the ones an established published clock uses, which is strong evidence it found something real rather than memorizing the data it was trained on.

Single-Cell Aging Pathway Analysis

Aging does not happen evenly across a body, or even across a single organ. This looks at a mouse lung one cell at a time, across 11 different cell types, and shows which ones change with age and how. The methodological point matters here: analyzing individual cells as if they were independent people is a common mistake that produces false results, so this treats each animal as the unit instead, which is the statistically correct approach.
